# BHUSA26 Network Performance Investigation: Single-Sheet Evidence Report

We spent two days trying to reproduce this problem, move it, and make it fail under controlled conditions. That work covered more than 250 tests or individual node/listener sessions across several conference locations, two client generations, TCP, UDP, HTTP/2, HTTP/3, multiple packet sizes and rates, and both wired and off-site controls. The distributed work inventoried 24 probes, established 21 trusted nodes, exercised 21 listeners across three XMission services, and sent 4,875 synchronized ICMP requests during the 64 KiB, 512 KiB, and maximum bidirectional runs. This isn't a general speed problem. The affected clients can download quickly or upload quickly when each direction runs alone. The failure appears when upload and download overlap. On the best 6 GHz laptop link, HTTP/3 download fell from 679.3 Mbps to 41.4 Mbps, a loss of 637.9 Mbps, while HTTP/2 still delivered 333.8 Mbps down and 394.9 Mbps up. Generic UDP and TCP reproduced the same downstream bias.

| Evidence area | What we measured | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reproduction | On a strong 6 GHz/80 MHz link at roughly -55 to -59 dBm, HTTP/3 delivered 679.3 Mbps down by itself and 41.4 Mbps with an upload running. Later repeats delivered 55.6 and 28.1 Mbps down. The HTTP/2 control between them delivered 317.0 Mbps. | We reproduced the complaint several times without moving the laptop or weakening the signal. The clearest HTTP/3 run kept only 6.1% of its directional download rate. |
| Generic UDP direction control | With both directions targeted at 350 Mbps, upload delivered 340.11 Mbps with no measured loss. Download delivered 273.45 Mbps with 20.771% loss. A later run delivered 348.24 Mbps up and 229.90 Mbps down with 33.534% downstream loss. Either direction alone was clean at about 350 Mbps. | The failure needs traffic in both directions and it consistently hurts download. HTTP/3 isn't required to trigger it. |
| Rate and packet-size fingerprint | With one direction fixed at 350 Mbps, downstream loss stayed near zero through 250 Mbps and climbed at 300 and 350 Mbps. At 350+350 Mbps, shrinking the UDP payload from 1,472 to 200 bytes raised downstream loss from 16.303% to 65.119%. | The 250-300 Mbps cliff and the sensitivity to packet rate look like airtime, scheduling, or queue pressure. A simple circuit-capacity limit doesn't fit. |
| Wired and external controls | On a 1 Gbps conference wired drop, simultaneous HTTP/3 kept 674.18 of 749.97 Mbps directional download, or 89.9%. Six 350+350 Mbps UDP buckets had zero downstream loss. The same laptop on MGM Wi-Fi didn't reproduce the HTTP/3 collapse. | The laptop and public test path can work normally. The trigger disappears when the affected conference WLAN is removed from the path. That points to wireless/controller processing or policy specific to the wireless VLAN. |
| MSS and path MTU | IPv4 packets at the 1,500-byte path limit succeeded, and high-rate TCP didn't hit a large-packet black hole. Black Hat MSS values changed by destination instead of showing one blanket clamp. MGM negotiated a smaller MSS but didn't reproduce the collapse. | MSS clamping and path MTU don't explain a failure that depends on traffic direction and concurrency. They aren't the primary cause. |
| Port, NAT, and dual uplinks | UDP ports 443, 444, and 445 all reproduced the problem. A live STUN mapping stayed stable during a failing run. Twenty source ports showed one stable client-visible identity, and fixed-port sweeps didn't split into healthy and bad groups. Test volume was well below the two 10 Gb uplinks. | This doesn't look like one blocked QUIC port, mid-session NAT rebinding, raw WAN exhaustion, or one obvious bad hash bucket. We still can't clear VLAN-specific firewall, NAT, egress, or uplink-member selection because wired and wireless used different public identities. |
| Distributed fixed-rate cohort | At 100 Mbps in each direction, 11 Wi-Fi 5/VHT probes averaged 27.02% downstream loss. For every 100 Mbps offered, about 27.02 Mbps was lost and 72.98 Mbps arrived. Eight Wi-Fi 6/HE probes averaged 0.98 Mbps lost and 99.02 Mbps received. Average upstream loss rates were 0.095% and 0%, respectively. Every client was on 5 GHz/40 MHz. | The downstream bias appears across rooms and VLANs and clusters around the older clients at a fixed rate. That is a useful fleet-level correlation, but it doesn't prove a Wi-Fi 7 compatibility or firmware bug. |
| Worst fleet nodes | PC10 lost 47.935 Mbps downstream per 100 Mbps offered, PC13 lost 42.965 Mbps, and PC3 lost 42.868 Mbps. Their follow-up radio snapshots were -52, -59, and -50 dBm on channels 157, 153, and 161. PC3 later delivered only 27.1 Mbps down against 122.1 Mbps up during maximum TCP stress, with 227 downstream retransmissions and local-gateway latency peaking at 67.252 ms. | PC10, PC13, and PC3 are the first clients to trace in AP and controller logs. Their good signal rules out weak RF as the shared explanation. PC3 has the best second set of evidence. |
| Cohort confounders and normalization | The older group used MediaTek MT7612U/mt76x2u, kernel 5.10, and Wi-Fi 5. The newer group used Intel AX200/AX210/iwlwifi, kernel 6.1, and Wi-Fi 6. No tested probe negotiated Wi-Fi 7/EHT. At rates scaled to usable capacity, PC6 lost 1.670% downstream at 60+60 Mbps and PV03 lost 0.578% at 125+125 Mbps. | Generation, adapter, driver, kernel, and usable PHY capacity all change together. Different airtime needs explain much of the fixed 100+100 split. The older clients still have worse downstream efficiency, but these tests can't assign that difference to Arista firmware. |
| TCP control | PC6 delivered 99.9 Mbps down by itself, then 69.8 and 61.0 Mbps during two 100+100 Mbps TCP runs. The downstream sender logged 76 and 56 retransmissions while upload stayed at 100 Mbps. PV03 kept 100-101 Mbps in both directions with no retransmissions. | TCP shows the same problem. Congestion control turns explicit UDP loss into a 30.1-38.9 Mbps drop in download rate, so this isn't limited to UDP, QUIC, or STUN. |
| Near-side latency | During PC6's 100+100 UDP failure, local-gateway average RTT rose from 1.646 to 7.146 ms and peaked at 22.738 ms while downstream loss reached 23.550%. PV03 moved from 2.340 to 2.975 ms with 0.400% downstream loss. Under maximum bidirectional stress, all six representative clients showed local-gateway latency increases, with one peak at 184.765 ms. Signal stayed stable and no gateway replies were lost. | Queueing is already visible on the client-facing wireless leg. The maximum test can starve download on either client generation, so we treat it as a bufferbloat stress result, not proof that healthy Wi-Fi 6 clients share the fixed-rate defect. |
| Waveform browser control | Chrome ran with one verified default route at a time. [Wired](https://www.waveform.com/tools/bufferbloat?test-id=134fe6c6-0762-4e54-88f2-1f6ae9542a3d) graded A+: 939.4 Mbps down, 929.2 Mbps up, 5 ms unloaded, +4 ms under download, and +0 ms under upload. [Wi-Fi](https://www.waveform.com/tools/bufferbloat?test-id=3808b1ad-8a65-4e7e-9302-db41b3925839) graded B: 806.7 Mbps down, 616.6 Mbps up, 8 ms unloaded, +32 ms under download, and +47 ms under upload. | Compared with wired, Wi-Fi added 28 ms more download-loaded latency and 47 ms more upload-loaded latency. It also delivered 132.7 Mbps less download and 312.6 Mbps less upload. This backs up the WLAN queueing finding in a normal browser. Waveform loads one direction at a time, so it doesn't replace the simultaneous iperf and gateway tests. |
| Public endpoint limits | The synchronized 21-listener run completed on the same 12 assignments with both 128 and 64 KiB blocks. Removing the nine repeat timeouts produced 12 of 12 completions at 512 KiB. Every port had passed a connection check before the larger fanout. | Those nine failures came from public-service admission limits, not clients producing zero throughput. We excluded them from WLAN conclusions. Public listeners need directional preflight and valid-duration checks before their results count. |
| Current fault boundary | The problem follows concurrent wireless load, favors downstream loss, reacts to packet rate, appears in UDP and TCP, affects distributed probes, raises local-gateway latency, and disappears in the wired and MGM controls. Host interface counters showed no new errors or drops. The privileged station retry counters were frozen, so their zero deltas aren't usable evidence. | The likely fault area is client-facing airtime and downlink scheduling, WMM queues, the controller tunnel/datapath, or an interaction with less-efficient legacy clients. The data doesn't support MSS/PMTU, QUIC alone, one UDP port, roaming, idle RF loss, raw 20 Gb WAN capacity, or the public server as the main cause. |

The problem is real: affected conference clients hit downstream queueing and starvation when upload and download overlap, and the delay is already visible on the wireless side of the path. We still can't tell whether that is a normal legacy-client airtime limit, C-460 scheduling, controller behavior, Wi-Fi 7 backward compatibility, or policy on the wireless VLAN. The next useful run is the same directional and simultaneous PC6/PV03 matrix against an internal wired iperf3 server. At the same time, capture each client’s AP and radio, retries, MCS, airtime, WMM queue drops, controller-tunnel counters, and AP-uplink counters. If the internal run fails while those AP or controller counters move, the public server, WAN, NAT, and dual uplinks are out of the picture. After that, test one matched adapter on a C-460 in normal Wi-Fi 7 mode and in a controlled non-BE or earlier-firmware configuration before calling this a firmware or backward-compatibility bug.
